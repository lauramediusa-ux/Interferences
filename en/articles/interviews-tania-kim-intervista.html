Beyond techno: Tania Kim and the art of surprising the dancefloor

A free path through jungle, techno, prog, electro and breakbeat: musical research, the residency at Tempio and the emergence of an identity able to turn every set into an unpredictable journey

Thomas Anderson
October 27, 2026
Photo by Hu Xin.
Interview

1. Before Tania Kim there is Tania Gianni: listener, enthusiast and music researcher. What was your first truly decisive encounter with electronic music, and when did you realise that watching the booth was no longer enough and that you wanted to build the musical path yourself?

I think I realised I wanted to build my own musical path back in my teenage years. We never listened to music at home, and I don’t blame my family for that: I come from a tiny village in central Italy where a culture of work, sacrifice and working the land leaves very little room for anything that could even vaguely be called a “whim”, like listening to or making music. I always felt different. And, a bit against the grain, I started wearing out records, playing bass and guitar, singing. I discovered electronic music when I was a little older: with my new friends from university we would go dancing in social centres and clubs all over Emilia-Romagna, chasing this DJ or that one. The first time I heard Drum and Bass on a club sound system it hit me like lightning (NOISIA at Link in Bologna – I never would have imagined that 10 years later we would share the stage on their Farewell Tour). I realised I wanted to do it too shortly afterwards, watching a DJ play records at CSO Capolinea in Faenza. There were fewer women back then, and her energy, technique and confidence behind the decks struck me deeply.

2. Your stage name, Tania Kim, already seems to suggest the birth of a further identity. How did the name come about, and what does it let you express that Tania Gianni, in everyday life, perhaps tends to keep to herself?

My stage name was the natural result of a nickname given to me by my “music mates” in my younger days. I was a slight girl with peroxide blonde hair and a vague resemblance to the bassist of Sonic Youth (Kim Gordon). Kim was simpler, shorter and more musical. Over time the two names merged, creating what is my alter ego: Tania Kim is the DJ behind the decks, the person you chat about music with at the club, the friend who helps you through a rough moment, the colleague you share the hard work with. I don’t feel her as an entity all that separate from the everyday Tania Gianni; both live on light and shadow and moments of contradiction.

3. Resident Advisor calls you a “jungle techno selectress”, yet in your sets you move through techno, trance, house, jungle, breakbeat and electro. In a scene that often asks artists to be instantly recognisable through a single genre, how important is it for you to defend the right to be unpredictable?

I could answer in a single word: ESSENTIAL. I believe a good selector is recognisable by the ability to navigate between different genres and styles while keeping a personal imprint. There’s no better feeling than surprising the dancefloor with something it doesn’t expect and watching it react positively. Unpredictability can be a signature too. It’s a process that still takes plenty of testing, mistakes, even bad experiences and a certain dose of frustration for not wanting to conform to the canons you mention. In a world that demands a sound recognisable from a few seconds of Instagram video and an image that is alluring but also a little bit intellectual, I prefer to be more eclectic, analogue and real.

Photo by Costantino Bedin.

4. Being a resident means knowing a dancefloor intimately over time, accompanying its transformations and building a deep relationship with a community. How has Tempio del Futuro Perduto allowed you to grow as an artist, and how much has its community helped define the sound you now recognise as yours?

I came to Tempio driven by a strong desire to immerse myself in its world and form a solid bond with its community. I didn’t end up there by chance: it was a conscious choice that had an enormous impact on my whole previous life, but it gave me back a certain measure of the artistic freedom I’d longed for so much. Compared with my earlier experiences as a resident DJ, I finally found a place that pushed me to mature seriously, both as a professional and as a human being. Tempio is, for me, a physical, spiritual and personal dimension. The community that moves through it is special; it stands out just as Tempio itself stands out from any other club. My sound has developed in a positive, identity-shaping way because I’ve been able to express more freely what I had inside. I also think that working in a healthy environment has let me understand far more clearly what I wanted to do and what I didn’t. I believe this first year of mine as a resident at Tempio has been truly important.

5. You’ve shared bills with very different figures, from Octa Octa and Eris Drew to Peaches, Boston 168 and DJ Nobu, engaging with languages that cross house, techno, punk, queer culture and experimentation. Which encounters marked you most, and what did you learn from watching artists with such radical paths?

I’ve been really lucky to meet and share the stage with so many important artists who are genuinely very different from one another. One of the encounters that marked me most is among those you mention: I had the chance to play the warm-up for Eris Drew and Octa Octa at the Dekmantel Club Tour at Link in Bologna. Besides being two great artists, they are extremely humble people and incredibly passionate about music. Talking with them I truly felt that, even though they’ve become major international artists, they’ve never lost that passion and curiosity that push you to take your first steps in this world. I think that’s a great lesson. Another person who intrigued me a lot is Ellen Alien. Even though she’s on a totally different “mood”, it was interesting to exchange opinions with such a great and important artist in the history of techno.

Photo by Gianluca La Neve.

6. With Ogni cosa EP, released by Italo Ghetto Records, your path enters a new phase: no longer just selecting and relating other people’s music, but generating a sonic universe that is entirely your own. What does this work tell, and which parts of your identity emerged during production that you couldn’t yet express in your DJ sets?

Ogni Cosa EP was composed, arranged and prepared for release in just a few months, even though for me it’s the end point of a journey that lasted a couple of years. Just as with DJing, in music production too I needed my own time to find the right path. This work aims to move away from the dancefloor to explore a more ethereal, emotional imagery, thanks to the words recited by my colleague and friend Silvia Fontana. The idea came to us during “Corpi Elettronici” (“Electronic Bodies”): an arts jam in which I’ve had the pleasure of collaborating a few times as a music selector, organised by Urban Dancing Prophets, the collective of dancers at Tempio. Producing music lets me express my creativity and my eclectic spirit to the fullest, which isn’t always possible when selecting other people’s music.

7. Today you are an Italian DJ and producer touring Europe, a resident in an independent space, within an electronic scene searching for new languages and new models. What kind of artist would you like to become in the coming years, and what would you like to leave in people when, with the lights and music over, they walk off the dancefloor after one of your sets?

I’d like people to feel the same sense of lightness I feel after turning down the volume on the last track of my DJ set, and to leave with the feeling, and the curiosity, of having discovered something new. I don’t care about fame, I don’t care about followers. I’d like to live off my art, improve technically and professionally, travel the world and be happy.
